# Phishing and Social Engineering Awareness Guide

## 1. What is social engineering?

Social engineering is manipulating people into giving away information, money or access. Instead of hacking
computers, attackers "hack" human nature: our wish to help, our respect for authority, our fear of getting into
trouble and our curiosity. More than 80% of the security incidents Corvane investigated last year started with a
message sent to an employee.

## 2. Common attack types

| Attack | Channel | Typical example |
|---|---|---|
| Phishing | Email | "Your mailbox is full. Log in here to keep receiving email." |
| Spear phishing | Email aimed at one person | A message that mentions your real project and manager. |
| CEO fraud / business email compromise | Email | An "executive" asks you to make an urgent, confidential payment. |
| Smishing | SMS / messaging apps | "Your parcel is held. Pay a €1.99 fee here." |
| Vishing | Phone call | A caller "from IT support" asks you to read out an MFA code. |
| Quishing | QR code | A QR code on a poster or in a PDF that leads to a fake login page. |
| Tailgating | In person | Someone without a badge follows you through a secure door. |

## 3. Spot it: the SLAM check

Before you click, open or reply, check **SLAM**:

- **S – Sender.** Look at the full email address, not just the display name. Watch for look-alike domains such as
  "corvane-support.com" or "c0rvane.example". All Corvane email comes from @corvane.example. External emails carry
  the yellow **[EXTERNAL]** banner.
- **L – Links.** Hover over a link to see where it really goes. Be suspicious of shortened links and of login pages
  that are not the Corvane SSO page.
- **A – Attachments.** Unexpected attachments, especially archives (.zip), macro-enabled Office files (.docm, .xlsm)
  and HTML files, are high risk.
- **M – Message.** Urgency, secrecy, threats, unusual requests and offers that seem too good to be true are classic
  pressure tactics.

## 4. Report it: the Report Phish button

If you think a message is suspicious, click **Report Phish** in Outlook (desktop, web or mobile). This sends the
message to the Security team and removes it from your inbox.

- Do **not** forward the suspicious email to colleagues to "warn them". This spreads the threat.
- Do not reply to the sender or click "unsubscribe".
- Report suspicious calls, texts and QR codes to the Security Desk (ext. 4444 or security@corvane.example).

You will never get into trouble for reporting a message that turns out to be legitimate. Reports are how the Security
team finds attacks early: the first report of a campaign usually allows the team to remove the message from every
mailbox within minutes.

## 5. Already clicked? Act fast

If you clicked a link, opened an attachment, entered your password or approved an MFA prompt you should not have:

1. **Call the Security Desk at ext. 4444 immediately**, at the latest within **15 minutes**. Speed is more important
   than anything else.
2. If you entered your password, change it from a different, trusted device.
3. Do not turn off your laptop; the Security team may need to examine it. Disconnect it from the network (switch off
   Wi-Fi) if the Security Desk tells you to.
4. Do not try to clean up or delete anything yourself.

People who report their own mistakes quickly are never disciplined for the click itself.

## 6. Payment and bank-detail changes

Fraudsters often pretend to be suppliers and ask Corvane to pay invoices to a "new" bank account. The rule is simple:

- Any request to change a supplier's bank details must be verified with a **call-back** to the phone number already
  stored in the supplier master data, **never** a phone number given in the request itself.
- Two people in Finance must approve the change (the "four-eyes" principle).
- Urgent payment requests from executives that bypass the normal process must be verified by calling the executive
  directly on their known number. Real executives will not mind.
- Corvane executives never ask employees to buy **gift cards**. Any such request is a scam.

## 7. Phone and in-person tricks

- IT staff will never ask for your password or an MFA code. Hang up and call the IT Service Desk (ext. 2222) yourself.
- Always wear your badge in the office and politely challenge people without a badge: "Can I help you find someone?"
  Visitors must be signed in at reception and escorted by their host at all times.
- Do not hold secure doors open for people you do not know. If someone insists, direct them to reception.
- Be careful with what you share on social media and networking sites. Attackers use job titles, team names and
  holiday plans to make messages more convincing.

## 8. Phishing simulations

The Security Awareness Team sends simulated phishing emails to all employees about **once a month**. If you report a
simulation with Report Phish, you receive a "well spotted" message. If you click a simulation, you are asked to
complete a 10-minute micro-learning module within **7 days**. Simulation results are used to improve training and
are never used for performance reviews.

## 9. Key takeaways

- Slow down: pressure and urgency are warning signs.
- Verify through a second, trusted channel.
- Report everything suspicious with Report Phish or ext. 4444.
- If you made a mistake, report it within 15 minutes.
